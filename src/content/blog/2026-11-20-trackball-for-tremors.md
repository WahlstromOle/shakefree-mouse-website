For many people with a hand tremor, a trackball is easier than a mouse. The trackball stays still on the desk, your hand rests on it, and **clicking doesn't move the pointer**. A mouse can still be the better choice if your fingers shake more than your arm, or if you can already steady a mouse well with your forearm on the desk. The only real test is to try one.

## How the two differ

**A mouse** moves the pointer when you move the whole device. Parkinson's UK notes the catch: the mouse has to stay still while you click. With a tremor, the press of the button often nudges the mouse, and the click lands beside the target.

**A trackball** doesn't move. You roll a ball with your thumb, fingers or palm, and the body of the device stays put. The University of Washington's DO-IT programme, which advises on technology and disability, calls trackballs a good first choice for people who lack the coordination for a standard mouse, for two reasons: the control surface is easier to handle, and the buttons can be pressed without moving the pointer.

There's a third benefit. Because the device doesn't travel, your hand and forearm can stay planted on the desk. A handout from the Shirley Ryan AbilityLab, a rehabilitation hospital, explains that supporting your arm joints on a stable surface reduces the impact of a tremor.

## When a trackball helps most

- **Your clicks miss** because the mouse shifts as you press.
- **Dragging is hard.** You can roll the ball, lift your fingers, and the pointer waits where you left it.
- **You have little desk space**, or use a tray or lap desk, since a trackball needs no room to move.
- **Your fingers are steadier than your arm.**

## When a mouse may be better

- **Your fingers or thumb shake most.** They're what moves the ball, so the shake may go straight into the pointer.
- **You already rest your forearm** and can hold a mouse fairly steady.
- **You can't get on with it.** AbilityNet, a UK technology charity, notes that a new kind of mouse often feels clumsy at first and takes a while to get used to. Give it a week or two before deciding, but not everyone takes to a trackball.

## Thumb or finger trackball?

Trackballs come in two main styles:

- **Thumb-operated:** shaped like a mouse, with a small ball at the side under your thumb. Your fingers click the buttons as usual.
- **Finger-operated:** a larger ball in the middle, rolled with your fingers or palm, with buttons around it.

AbilityNet's factsheet says that **larger trackballs are easier to control** and suit people with limited fine motor control, including people with tremors. Many people find a big ball, moved with several fingers or the palm, easier to keep steady than a small ball under one thumb. If your thumb is steadier than your fingers, though, a thumb trackball may suit you. Left-handed? Check the shape before buying, because some trackballs are made for the right hand only.

## What to look for

- **A ball size that suits your steadiest movement** (see above).
- **Large, separate buttons** you can press without touching the ball.
- **A drag or "click-and-hold" button.** DO-IT notes that some trackballs have extra buttons for double-clicking or click-and-hold, which you can set up to suit you.
- **A heavy, non-slip base**, so resting your hand doesn't shift it.
- **A way to connect that suits you.** Parkinson's UK notes that trackballs can plug into a laptop by cable or a small wireless receiver.
- **An easy return policy.** What works is very personal.

## Setting up a trackball in Windows

Windows treats a trackball as a mouse, so the same settings apply:

1. Open **Settings** (press the Windows key and I together).
2. Go to **Bluetooth & devices** > **Mouse**.
3. Lower **Mouse pointer speed** a few steps. A slower pointer means a small shake of the ball moves the pointer less.
4. Click **Additional mouse settings**. On the **Buttons** tab, move **Double-click speed** towards **Slow**.

If your trackball has no drag button, [ClickLock](/clicklock-windows-11) does the same job: hold the button briefly to lock it, roll to drag, and click to let go.

## If the pointer still shakes

A trackball changes how the shake gets in; it doesn't remove it. If the pointer still jitters, [mouse smoothing software](/mouse-stabilizer-software) can filter the shake out of the movement, and it works with a trackball just as it does with a mouse. That's what our app, [ShakeFree Mouse](https://apps.microsoft.com/detail/9NXR1GRZ2P77), does; you can [try the demo](/#demo) in your browser.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a trackball better than a mouse for essential tremor?**
Often, but not always. A trackball helps most when clicking moves your mouse off target. If your fingers shake more than your arm, a mouse with a well-supported forearm may work better.

**How long does it take to get used to a trackball?**
It varies. Expect it to feel clumsy at first. Use it for everyday tasks for a week or two before you decide.

**Can I keep my mouse plugged in as well?**
Yes. Windows lets you use both at once, so you can switch between them for different tasks.

**Does a trackball stop the pointer shaking?**
No. It makes clicks land more reliably and lets your hand rest, but shaking fingers still move the ball.

## A note on your health

A trackball makes the computer easier to use; it doesn't treat a tremor. If your tremor is new, getting worse or affecting daily life, talk to your doctor. An occupational therapist can let you try different devices and advise on your set-up.
