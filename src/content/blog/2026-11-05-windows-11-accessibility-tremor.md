The quickest way in is to press the **Windows key and U** together. That opens **Settings** > **Accessibility** directly. The settings that matter most for a tremor sit under the heading **Interaction**, further down the page.

If you haven't yet changed your mouse settings, start with our guide [How to make your mouse easier to use with a hand tremor](/mouse-hand-tremor), which covers pointer speed, pointer size and double-click speed. This guide covers the rest of Windows: the keyboard, your voice, and a few settings that are easy to miss.

## Filter keys: stop keys being typed twice

If your hand shakes while you type, you have probably seen "thhe" or "ssee" appear on screen. **Filter keys** makes the keyboard ignore key presses that are too short or that repeat too fast, so one shaky press counts as one letter.

1. Press the **Windows key and U**.
2. Under **Interaction**, select **Keyboard**.
3. Turn on **Filter keys**, then select the arrow next to it to see its options.

There are three options inside. You don't need all of them, so try one at a time:

- **Ignore unintended keystrokes** (also called Bounce keys): after you press a key, Windows waits a moment before it will accept the same key again. This is usually the one that fixes doubled letters.
- **Ignore quick keystrokes** (Slow keys): a key has to be held down briefly before it counts. This helps if your fingers brush keys by accident on the way to the one you want.
- **Ignore repeated keystrokes** (Repeat keys): slows down how fast a held key repeats, so resting on a key doesn't fill the line.

Each option has a drop-down where you set the waiting time. Start with a short time and make it longer only if you still get mistakes, because a long wait can make normal typing feel sluggish.

<!-- SCREENSHOT: the Filter keys options expanded, showing the three switches and their drop-downs -->

One thing to know: on many PCs, holding the right **Shift** key for about eight seconds turns Filter keys on or off. If your typing suddenly seems to ignore you, that shortcut may be why. You can switch the shortcut off on the same page.

## Sticky keys: shortcuts one key at a time

Shortcuts such as **Ctrl and C** (copy) save a lot of clicking, but pressing two or three keys at once is hard with a shaky hand. **Sticky keys** lets you press them one after another instead: press **Ctrl**, let go, then press **C**.

1. Press the **Windows key and U**.
2. Under **Interaction**, select **Keyboard**.
3. Turn on **Sticky keys**.

Pressing **Shift** five times in a row also turns Sticky keys on. If a box pops up asking about Sticky keys when you didn't mean it, that's why; you can switch off that shortcut in the Sticky keys options.

## Voice access: click and type by speaking

On a shaky day, saying "click Start" can be far easier than aiming at it. **Voice access** lets you open apps, click buttons and dictate text with your voice. It's built into Windows 11 (version 22H2 and later), and once your language is downloaded it works without an internet connection.

1. Press the **Windows key and U**.
2. Under **Interaction**, select **Speech**.
3. Turn on **Voice access**. The first time, Windows downloads the speech files and asks which microphone to use.

A bar appears at the top of the screen. A few commands to start with:

- **"Click"** and the name of a button, for example "click File".
- **"Show numbers"** puts a number on everything you can click. Say the number to click it. This is a big help on pages full of small links.
- **"Show grid"** divides the screen into numbered squares. Say a number to zoom in, again and again, until the pointer is exactly where you want it. It works well together with [Mouse keys](/mouse-keys-windows-11) for the last small nudge.

Voice access has an interactive guide that teaches the basics. Microsoft lists the supported languages on its help pages; at the time of writing they include English, French, German and Spanish.

## Make the text cursor easy to find

When you're typing in a document, the thin blinking line (the text cursor) is easy to lose, and a shaky click can drop it in the wrong place. Two settings make it much easier to see where you're typing:

1. Press the **Windows key and U** and select **Text cursor**.
2. Turn on **Text cursor indicator**. It adds a coloured marker to the cursor. You can choose its size and colour.
3. Drag the **Text cursor thickness** slider to the right to make the line itself thicker.

## The On-screen keyboard: type with the pointer

If pressing physical keys is harder than pointing, the **On-screen keyboard** shows a keyboard you can click instead. Turn it on under **Accessibility** > **Keyboard**, or press **Windows key, Ctrl and O** together. Many people use it only now and then, for example for a password on a day when the tremor is worse.

## Where to start

You don't need to change everything at once. A good order to try:

1. **Filter keys** with **Ignore unintended keystrokes**, if you get doubled letters.
2. **Sticky keys**, if keyboard shortcuts are awkward.
3. **Voice access**, for the clicks that are hardest to aim.

Give each change a day or two before deciding whether it helps. What works is very personal, and it's fine to switch something back off.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will these settings affect other people who use my PC?**
They're saved for your own Windows account. Someone signing in with their own account keeps their own settings.

**Filter keys made my typing feel slow. What should I do?**
Shorten the waiting time in its drop-down, or turn off **Ignore quick keystrokes** and keep only **Ignore unintended keystrokes**.

**Do I need to install anything?**
No. Everything in this guide is built into Windows 11.

**My settings look different or are greyed out.**
Menus can change a little between Windows updates. If options are greyed out on a work PC, your organisation may manage them, so ask your IT team.

## A note on your health

These settings make the computer easier to use; they don't treat a tremor. If your tremor is new, changing, or making everyday tasks harder, talk to your doctor. An occupational therapist can also suggest equipment and ways of working that suit you.
