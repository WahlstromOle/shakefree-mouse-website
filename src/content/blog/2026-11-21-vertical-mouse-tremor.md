A vertical mouse helps some people with a tremor, but it isn't designed for tremor and there's no good evidence either way. It was made to ease **wrist and forearm strain**. It can still be worth trying, because it changes which muscles move the pointer and how your arm rests. Just buy one you can return.

## What a vertical mouse is

A vertical mouse stands upright, so you hold it with your palm facing sideways, as if shaking someone's hand. The buttons sit on the side, under your fingers, and the wheel is between them.

AbilityNet, a UK charity that advises on technology and disability, describes two things this changes:

- It turns the wrist to a more natural **"handshake" position**.
- It moves the work away from the wrist and onto the **larger shoulder muscles**.

AbilityNet describes vertical mice as more comfortable for some people. Neither AbilityNet nor the tremor and Parkinson's organisations we checked recommend them for tremor specifically, and we couldn't find a study that tested a vertical mouse with people who have a tremor. So what follows is about what to expect and what to test, not a promise.

## Why it might help

- **Bigger muscles do the steering.** Because you move a vertical mouse more from the arm and shoulder than from the wrist and fingers, a tremor that is mainly in your wrist or fingers may reach the pointer less.
- **Your forearm rests on its side.** Many people find that the outer edge of the hand and forearm settle firmly on the desk in this position. A supported arm matters: a handout from the Shirley Ryan AbilityLab, a rehabilitation hospital, explains that resting your arm joints on a stable surface reduces the impact of a tremor.
- **A fuller grip.** Your whole hand wraps around the mouse, which some people find easier to hold steady than a low, flat mouse held with the fingertips.

## Why it might not

- **You still have to hold it still to click.** Parkinson's UK notes that the difficulty with any mouse is keeping it still while you press. A vertical mouse doesn't change that.
- **Clicks push sideways.** On a flat mouse you press down into the desk. On a vertical mouse you squeeze sideways, so check whether your clicks nudge the mouse. Some people find a firm thumb on the other side cancels it out; others find it worse than a flat mouse.
- **It takes getting used to.** AbilityNet notes that any new kind of mouse can feel clumsy at first.
- **If your tremor is in the whole arm,** moving the work to the arm and shoulder won't steady the pointer.

## How to test one fairly

1. **Buy from a shop with an easy return policy,** or borrow one. If you see an occupational therapist, ask whether they have one to try.
2. **Check the fit.** AbilityNet notes that ergonomic mice come in different sizes and in left- and right-handed versions. Your hand should rest on it without stretching or gripping hard.
3. **Set your arm up first.** Forearm on the desk, mouse close to your body, shoulders relaxed.
4. **Use it for at least a week** for ordinary tasks before you judge it.
5. **Test the things that matter to you:** clicking a small button, double-clicking a file, dragging a window. Compare honestly with your old mouse.
6. **Keep your Windows settings.** A slower pointer and an easier double-click help with any mouse; our [guide to using a mouse with a hand tremor](/mouse-hand-tremor) has the steps.

AbilityNet also mentions **adjustable ergonomic mice**, which are hinged so they can sit anywhere between flat and vertical. If fully upright feels strange, an angle in between may suit you better.

## Vertical mouse or trackball?

If your main problem is that **clicks land in the wrong place**, a trackball is usually the first thing to try, because the device stays still when you press a button. We compare the two in [Trackball vs mouse for a hand tremor](/trackball-for-tremors).

If your main problem is **an aching wrist or forearm** on top of the tremor, a vertical mouse is aimed at exactly that.

Either way, the pointer may still shake. Free Windows settings, a supported arm and, if needed, smoothing software deal with that part; our [overview of assistive technology for hand tremors](/assistive-technology-for-tremors) shows how the pieces fit together.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a vertical mouse good for essential tremor?**
It helps some people and not others. It isn't designed for tremor, and we found no research testing it for tremor. Try one you can return.

**Is a vertical mouse better than a trackball for shaky hands?**
They solve different problems. A trackball stays still while you click, which often helps with accuracy. A vertical mouse is mainly about wrist comfort.

**Do vertical mice come in left-handed versions?**
Ergonomic mice are made in left- and right-handed versions, and most are shaped for one hand only, so check which version you're buying.

**How long does it take to get used to one?**
It varies from person to person. Give it at least a week of everyday use.

## A note on your health

A different mouse can make the computer more comfortable; it doesn't treat a tremor. If your tremor is new, getting worse or affecting daily life, talk to your doctor. An occupational therapist can advise on equipment and on how you sit and work.
