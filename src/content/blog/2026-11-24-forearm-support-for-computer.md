Supporting your forearm is one of the simplest ways to make a tremor matter less at the computer. When the arm rests on a stable surface, the hand has a steady base and less of the shake reaches the mouse or keys. Start with **the desk and chair you already have**; if that isn't enough, a **wrist rest**, a **clamp-on arm support** or a **lap tray** can fill the gap.

## Why support helps

An arm held in the air has nothing to steady it. Rest the arm, and that changes.

- A handout on computer use and tremor from the Shirley Ryan AbilityLab, a rehabilitation hospital in Chicago, explains that when your arm joints are supported on a stable surface, the impact of a tremor is reduced, and that good posture can make the computer easier to use.
- An article for the International Essential Tremor Foundation, written by a neurologist and an occupational therapist about children with essential tremor, describes "stabilisation" as one of the main approaches therapists use: leaning on a surface, or steadying things with non-slip mats. It notes that children often discover for themselves that leaning on something helps.

Many adults with a tremor find the same: the hand is calmer when the forearm is planted.

## Start with your desk and chair

Before buying anything, get the basics right. AbilityNet, a UK charity that advises on technology and disability, and Parkinson's UK both stress posture and positioning.

1. **Sit close** enough that your forearms can lie on the desk, not just your wrists.
2. **Set the chair height** so your shoulders are relaxed and your elbows are bent at about a right angle.
3. **Use the armrests** if your chair has them and they're level with the desk. If they stop you getting close, the desk may be the better support.
4. **Bring the mouse and keyboard close.** AbilityNet advises keeping the mouse near the centre of your body so you aren't over-stretching; a compact keyboard helps with that.
5. **Put the screen straight ahead,** about an arm's length away, with the top roughly at eye level, so you aren't twisting or leaning.
6. **Steady a laptop.** Put it on a table or desk, not your knees. If you raise it on a stand, AbilityNet notes you'll usually need a separate keyboard and mouse.

Small, cheap fixes count. The University of Washington's DO-IT programme mentions wood blocks under a table and a box under a keyboard as low-cost ways to get heights right.

## Wrist rests and pads

A wrist rest is a padded strip that sits in front of the keyboard or mouse. AbilityNet describes arm and wrist pads as somewhere to rest between bouts of typing, which can reduce discomfort and strain.

For a tremor, people use them a little differently: as an anchor for the heel of the hand while the fingers work. Many people find that helps with the mouse in particular. Things to check:

- **Height.** The pad should be level with the front of the keyboard or mouse, so your wrist isn't bent up or down.
- **Firmness.** Many people find a firm pad steadier than a very soft one.
- **Grip.** It shouldn't slide when you lean on it.

AbilityNet also mentions wrist "donuts": padded rings worn like a bracelet, which give support wherever your hand goes.

A folded towel does the same job well enough to tell you whether a wrist rest is worth buying.

## Clamp-on arm supports

If the desk is too shallow, or holding your arms up is tiring, an arm support takes over. AbilityNet describes **articulating arm supports** that clamp to the desk and suit people who have difficulty supporting the weight of their arms. Typically your forearm lies on a padded rest that moves with you, so you can still reach across the keyboard or move the mouse.

They're worth a look if:

- your desk has no room in front of the keyboard for your forearms
- your arm tires quickly, and the tremor gets worse as it does
- you use a mouse over a wide area.

These cost more than a wrist rest and need fitting to your desk, so they're a good thing to try with an occupational therapist before buying.

## Lap trays

If you use a laptop or tablet in an armchair or in bed, the device itself wobbles, which adds to the problem. AbilityNet describes lap trays with a beanbag base that moulds to your legs and gives a flat, stable surface. Cushions under your elbows add support for the arms.

## Other ways to steady your hands

- **A keyguard** gives your hands a plate to rest on while you type. See [Keyguards: what they are and who they help](/keyguard-keyboard).
- **A trackball** stays in one place, so your forearm can stay planted while your fingers move the pointer. See [Trackball vs mouse for a hand tremor](/trackball-for-tremors).
- **A non-slip mat** under the keyboard stops it creeping away as you press.

## Getting advice

An occupational therapist can look at how you sit and work. Parkinson's UK notes that they can suggest simple changes, such as posture, chair height, or the distance between keyboard and screen, and that you can usually be referred through your GP or social services. DO-IT makes the same point: specialist equipment is of little use if you aren't positioned well enough to control it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does resting your arm reduce a tremor?**
It doesn't change the tremor itself, but many people find the hand is steadier, and less of the shake reaches the mouse or keys, when the forearm is supported.

**Is a wrist rest or a forearm support better?**
Try the desk first, then a wrist rest, which is cheap. A clamp-on forearm support is the next step if your arm tires or your desk has no room to rest on.

**Are chair armrests good enough?**
Often, if they're level with the desk and let you sit close. If they keep you away from the desk, rest your forearms on the desk instead.

**Can I use a support with a laptop?**
Yes. Put the laptop on a desk, or use a lap tray. A separate keyboard and mouse make it easier to set everything at a comfortable height.

## A note on your health

Supports make the computer easier to use; they don't treat a tremor. If you have pain, numbness or tingling in your hand or arm, or your tremor is new, getting worse or affecting daily life, talk to your doctor.
