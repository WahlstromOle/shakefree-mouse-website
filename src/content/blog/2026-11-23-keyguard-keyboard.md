A keyguard is a rigid plate that sits over a keyboard, with a hole above each key. Your finger has to go **through a hole** to press a key, so it can't slide onto the key next door or press two at once, and you can **rest your hands on the plate** without typing anything. Keyguards mostly help people with tremor or poor hand control who hit the wrong keys.

## How a keyguard works

On an ordinary keyboard the keys are a few millimetres apart and level with each other, so a shaking finger easily catches two keys or lands between them. A keyguard puts a raised wall around every key.

The organisations that advise on this describe the same two benefits:

- **Fewer wrong keys.** The University of Washington's DO-IT programme describes a keyguard as a plastic or metal shield with holes, so that someone with limited dexterity or hand control presses only the key they mean to.
- **Somewhere to rest.** Parkinson's UK and AbilityNet both point out that you can rest your hands and arms on the guard without pressing any keys.

That second point is easy to overlook. With a tremor, hovering your hands above the keys is tiring and makes the shake worse for many people. With a keyguard, your hand settles on the plate, and one finger dips into a hole.

## Who a keyguard helps

A keyguard is worth considering if:

- you often **press two keys at once**, or the key beside the one you wanted
- your hand **drags across the keys** on the way to the one you want
- you need to **steady your hand** on something before each press
- you type with **one or two fingers**, or with a pointer or stylus.

It's less likely to suit you if you **touch-type quickly**, because reaching into a hole for every letter slows that style of typing down. And it doesn't solve **doubled letters** caused by a finger bouncing on the right key. That's a job for a Windows setting (see below).

## The drawbacks

Be realistic about the trade-offs:

- **Typing is slower and more deliberate.** For many people who make a lot of mistakes, that is still faster overall, because there's less to correct.
- **Key combinations are awkward.** Holding Shift or Ctrl while pressing another key is harder through holes. [Sticky Keys](/sticky-keys-windows-11) fixes this by letting you press the keys one after another.
- **It has to fit your keyboard.** Key positions differ between models, so a keyguard is made for a particular keyboard. AbilityNet's advice is that it's usually easier to buy a keyboard and keyguard together than to find a guard for a keyboard you already own.
- **Laptops are harder.** Laptop keyboards vary so much that it's often simpler to plug in a separate keyboard with its own guard.

One reassurance from AbilityNet: a keyguard can be taken off and put back, so it can be fitted only when it's needed. That matters if you share the computer.

## Try the free settings first

Before you buy anything, switch on the Windows settings that tackle the same problems:

- [Filter Keys](/filter-keys-windows-11) can ignore brief, accidental presses and doubled letters. For some people this alone is enough.
- [Sticky Keys](/sticky-keys-windows-11) removes the need to hold two keys at once.
- [Text suggestions](/text-suggestions-windows-11) and [voice typing](/voice-typing-windows-11) cut down how many keys you press in the first place.

If you still land on the wrong keys with those switched on, a keyguard tackles what the settings can't: where your finger physically lands. The two work well together.

## How to choose one

1. **Decide on the keyboard first.** If large keys would help you too, look for a large-key keyboard sold with a matching keyguard. Our guide to [keyboards for hand tremors](/keyboard-for-hand-tremors) covers the options.
2. **Match the exact model and layout.** A UK-layout guard won't fit a US-layout keyboard, and the Enter key is a different shape on each.
3. **Check how it attaches.** It should sit firmly and not rattle or lift when you rest your hands on it.
4. **Look at the hole size.** Your finger, or the pointer you type with, should pass through easily without catching.
5. **Ask before you buy.** An occupational therapist may be able to let you try one. In the UK, AbilityNet's free helpline (0300 180 0028) can advise on suppliers.

## Getting used to it

- **Rest first, then press.** Let your hand settle on the plate, then move one finger to the hole.
- **Slow down on purpose** for the first few days. Accuracy comes first; speed follows.
- **Keep Filter Keys on** while you learn, so a bounce inside the hole doesn't type a letter twice.
- **Set your arms up well.** A keyguard works best when your forearms are supported; see [typing with a hand tremor](/typing-with-hand-tremor).

For other tools that help at the keyboard and the mouse, see our [overview of assistive technology for hand tremors](/assistive-technology-for-tremors).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a keyguard on a keyboard?**
A rigid plastic or metal plate that fits over the keys, with a hole above each one. It guides your finger to a single key and gives your hands somewhere to rest.

**Do keyguards help with tremors?**
They help many people whose tremor makes them hit the wrong key or two keys at once. They don't stop doubled letters from a bouncing finger; Filter Keys handles that.

**Will a keyguard fit my keyboard?**
Only if it's made for that model. It's usually easiest to buy the keyboard and keyguard together.

**Does a keyguard slow down typing?**
Each key press is a little slower, but if you currently make many mistakes, you may finish sooner because you correct less.

## A note on your health

A keyguard makes typing easier; it doesn't treat a tremor. If your tremor is new, getting worse or affecting daily life, talk to your doctor. They can also refer you to an occupational therapist, who can advise on equipment.
