Assistive technology for tremors falls into seven groups: **settings already built into your computer**, **different pointing devices** such as trackballs, **keyboards and keyguards**, **arm and wrist supports**, **voice control**, **software that steadies the pointer**, and **hands-free options** such as eye tracking. Most people do best with a mix of two or three. Start with the free ones.

## 1. Settings you already have

Windows has settings made for exactly the problems a tremor causes, and they cost nothing:

- **For the mouse:** a slower, bigger pointer, an easier double-click, and ClickLock for dragging. Our [guide to using a mouse with a hand tremor](/mouse-hand-tremor) walks through them.
- **For the keyboard:** [Filter Keys](/filter-keys-windows-11) ignores doubled and accidental presses, and [Sticky Keys](/sticky-keys-windows-11) lets you press shortcuts one key at a time.
- **For typing less:** [text suggestions](/text-suggestions-windows-11) and [keyboard shortcuts](/windows-keyboard-shortcuts).

Our overview of [Windows 11 accessibility settings for hand tremors](/windows-11-accessibility-tremor) lists the rest. Try these first: they show you which problems are left for equipment to solve.

## 2. A different pointing device

A standard mouse asks a lot of a shaky hand. Parkinson's UK points out that a mouse has to be kept still while you click, which is the hard part. Other devices change that:

- **Trackballs.** The device stays still and you roll a ball with your thumb, fingers or palm. The University of Washington's DO-IT programme calls trackballs a good first choice for people who find a standard mouse hard to control, partly because the buttons can be pressed without moving the pointer. AbilityNet, a UK charity that helps disabled people use technology, notes that larger trackballs are easier to control and suit people with tremors.
- **Joysticks.** You push a stick to steer the pointer, much like the control on an electric wheelchair.
- **Vertical and ergonomic mice.** These are shaped for comfort, with the hand in a "handshake" position.
- **Bar mice**, which sit in front of the keyboard, and **touchpads**.

If you use a laptop, our [touchpad settings for shaky hands](/touchpad-settings-tremor) may help. And if the mouse is the problem only some of the time, [Mouse Keys](/mouse-keys-windows-11) moves the pointer from the keyboard.

## 3. Keyboards and keyguards

Keyboards with larger keys give your fingers a bigger target. A **keyguard** is a rigid plate with a hole over each key: it stops you pressing two keys at once, and you can rest your hands on it. Our guide to [keyboards for hand tremors](/keyboard-for-hand-tremors) covers what to look for, and [typing with a hand tremor](/typing-with-hand-tremor) covers technique and settings.

## 4. Arm and wrist supports

How you sit matters as much as what you hold. A handout on computer use and tremor from the Shirley Ryan AbilityLab, a rehabilitation hospital in Chicago, explains that when your arm joints rest on a stable surface, a tremor has less effect.

Sometimes the desk itself is enough. AbilityNet also describes:

- **arm or wrist pads** that sit in front of the keyboard
- **articulating arm supports** that clamp to the desk and take the weight of your arm
- **lap trays** with a beanbag base, for a steadier laptop or tablet on your knees.

## 5. Your voice

If your voice is clear, it can take over much of the typing and some of the clicking. Mayo Clinic lists speech-recognition software on a computer, and voice commands on a phone, among the practical steps for living with essential tremor.

In Windows 11, [voice typing](/voice-typing-windows-11) turns speech into text in any text box, and voice access lets you open apps and click buttons by speaking. Many people mix voice with a keyboard and mouse, using each for what it does best.

## 6. Software that steadies the pointer

Settings make shaking matter less; smoothing software goes further and filters the shake out of the pointer's movement. It works with whatever mouse, trackball or touchpad you have. Our article on [mouse smoothing software for tremors](/mouse-stabilizer-software) explains how it works, what research has found, and its limits.

This is what we make. [ShakeFree Mouse](https://apps.microsoft.com/detail/9NXR1GRZ2P77) is a small Windows app that smooths the pointer, keeps it still while you click, and ignores accidental second clicks. You can [try the demo](/#demo) in your browser, and the app is free to try for 5 days.

AbilityNet also mentions dwell-click software, which clicks for you when the pointer rests in one place, so you don't press a button at all.

## 7. Hands-free options

If your hands can't manage a pointing device at all, the pointer can follow another part of your body:

- **Head tracking** moves the pointer as you move your head, using a camera or a small wearable device.
- **Eye tracking** moves the pointer to where you look, and can click when your gaze rests on one spot. Windows 11 has eye control built in; Microsoft notes that the eye-tracking hardware is sold separately.
- **Switches** are large buttons, or sensors, that step through choices on screen.

These take more setting up, and most people with a tremor never need them. It's good to know they exist.

## Where to start

1. **Change the free settings** and use them for a few days.
2. **Sort out your seat and desk** so your forearms are supported.
3. **Note what still goes wrong.** Missed small targets? Accidental double-clicks? Wrong keys?
4. **Match a tool to that problem**, one at a time. Buy from somewhere with an easy return policy, because what works is very personal.

## Who can help

An occupational therapist can assess how you work and suggest equipment. Mayo Clinic notes that occupational therapists help people adapt to living with essential tremor, and Parkinson's UK says you can usually be referred to one through your GP or social services. In the UK, AbilityNet runs a free helpline (0300 180 0028) and can arrange a volunteer to help at home.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the best assistive technology for hand tremors?**
There isn't one best tool. It depends on which tasks give you trouble. Free settings and a supported arm help most people; after that, a trackball, a keyguard, voice typing or smoothing software each solve different problems.

**Is assistive technology expensive?**
Much of it is free, because it's built into Windows. Equipment varies widely in price, so start with the free settings, and try before you buy where you can.

**Can I combine several tools?**
Yes, and most people do: for example, a trackball, a slower pointer and voice typing for long emails.

**Does assistive technology treat a tremor?**
No. It makes tasks easier; it doesn't change the tremor itself. Treatment is something to discuss with your doctor.

## A note on your health

Nothing here is medical advice. The NHS advises seeing a GP if a tremor is getting worse over time or is affecting your daily activities. Your doctor can check for a cause, discuss treatment, and refer you to an occupational therapist.
