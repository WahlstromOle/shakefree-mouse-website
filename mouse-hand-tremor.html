How to make your mouse easier to use with a hand tremor

25 October 2026 · 6 minute read

A shaky hand turns small things into hard work: hitting a tiny close button, clicking a link without slipping off it, or double-clicking without opening the wrong file. The good news is that a few changes, most of them free and already built into Windows, can make a big difference.

Here’s what tends to help most, starting with the quickest things to try.

1. Slow the pointer down

When the pointer moves fast, every small shake becomes a big jump on screen. Slowing it down makes the same shake move the pointer less.

  1. Open Settings (press the Windows key and I together).
  2. Go to Bluetooth & devices > Mouse.
  3. Move Mouse pointer speed a few steps to the left and try it for a day.

On the same page, click Additional mouse settings, open the Pointer Options tab and try switching Enhance pointer precision off and on. It makes the pointer travel further when you move quickly. Some people with tremor find it easier with it off, others with it on, so test both.

2. Make the pointer and everything else bigger

A bigger pointer is easier to follow, and bigger buttons are easier to hit.

  • Pointer size and colour: Settings > Accessibility > Mouse pointer and touch. A larger size and a bright colour help you see where the pointer is at a glance.
  • Find the pointer quickly: in Additional mouse settings > Pointer Options, tick Show location of pointer when I press the CTRL key.
  • Larger text: Settings > Accessibility > Text size.
  • Larger everything: Settings > System > Display > Scale. Raising it makes buttons, icons and menus bigger targets.

In your web browser, you can also press Ctrl and + to zoom in on a page, and Ctrl and 0 to go back to normal.

3. Make double-clicking and dragging easier

Double-clicks and drags are where a tremor gets in the way most. In Additional mouse settings, on the Buttons tab:

  • Double-click speed: move it towards Slow, so you have more time for the second click.
  • ClickLock: when it’s on, you hold the button down briefly to “lock” it, move the mouse to drag, and click again to let go. You don’t have to keep the button pressed while you move.

You can also avoid double-clicking altogether: click a file once to select it, then press Enter to open it.

4. Steady your arm, not just your hand

Tremor often gets smaller when your arm has something solid to rest on.

  • Rest your forearm on the desk or a chair armrest, rather than letting your arm hover.
  • Keep the mouse close to your body, so you aren’t reaching.
  • Try a wrist rest or a folded towel under your wrist.
  • Sit so your elbow is bent at about a right angle and your shoulders are relaxed.

5. Try a different mouse

The mouse itself matters. Things people with tremor often find helpful:

  • A heavier mouse. Extra weight can damp small shakes. Some gaming mice let you add weights.
  • A trackball. The trackball stays still on the desk and you roll the ball with your thumb or fingers, so your hand can rest on it while you click.
  • A larger mouse that fills your hand, which some people find easier to hold steady.

If you can, try one before you buy, or buy from a shop with an easy return policy. What works is very personal.

6. Use the keyboard and your voice for some tasks

Every click you don’t need is one less chance to miss. A few keyboard shortcuts replace a lot of fiddly clicking:

  • Tab moves between buttons and boxes, and Enter or Space presses them.
  • Alt and F4 closes the window you’re in.
  • Ctrl and C copies, Ctrl and V pastes, Ctrl and Z undoes.

Windows 11 also has Voice access (Settings > Accessibility > Speech), which lets you open apps, click buttons and type by speaking. And Mouse keys (Settings > Accessibility > Mouse) lets you move the pointer with the number keys, one small step at a time.

A note on your health

These tips are about making the computer easier to use. They aren’t medical advice and they don’t treat a tremor. If your tremor is new, getting worse, or affecting daily life, talk to your doctor. An occupational therapist can also suggest equipment and ways of working that suit you.
